The slow death of affordable gaming

Gaming spent fifty years going from an expensive niche hobby to one of the cheapest forms of entertainment around. The AI boom is starting to push it back the other way.

Growing up, video games were my escape from reality. My friends all had the PlayStation 2, which launched at $299 in 20001. My parents couldn’t afford one, so I had the older, cheaper Nintendo 64, which Nintendo had cut to $99 the year before2. I spent hours exploring the world of Ocarina of Time, making ridiculous tackles in NFL Blitz and causing chaos in WrestleMania 2000.

Gaming has stayed part of my life as I’ve gotten older. Adult responsibilities take up more of my time now, but I still try to find a few hours to relax with a game.

Over the past few years, though, I’ve noticed a trend across the industry: hardware keeps getting more expensive. (I’m using U.S. prices throughout.) Consoles that should be getting cheaper as they age are going up in price instead. It made me wonder why, and what it means for the next kid whose parents can only afford the cheaper console.

How gaming became affordable

Home gaming started out as a luxury. The Atari 2600 cost $199 in 1977, more than $1,000 in today’s money3. Over the next few decades, Nintendo, Sony, Microsoft and the home PC put games in millions of living rooms. Cheap older consoles, secondhand discs and eventually phones opened the hobby to almost every income level.

Today, 212.3 million Americans play video games every week, about 67% of people aged 5 to 90, and 83% of U.S. households played on at least one device in the past year4. Worldwide, Newzoo expects 3.7 billion players and $213.9 billion in revenue in 20265.

Consoles used to get cheaper

My Nintendo 64 was cheap for a reason: consoles used to drop in price as they got older. Every few years, chipmakers could shrink a console’s processor so more of them fit on a single silicon wafer, which made each one cheaper to build. Console makers passed the savings on to win over buyers who had waited.

That cycle is how families like mine got in. You didn’t buy the new console at launch. You waited a few years, or you bought the one your friends were trading up from.

When Reddit’s r/gaming community discussed this year’s price increases, one user described the same history:

“I remember when my parents told me how expensive the first game consoles were because they werent household items. They were for the rich kids. Then the prices became managable and everyone had a Nintendo and a PS1. After a few years you would see em sold for about $100 to $200 LESS than what it was bought for because electronics fail overtime and better electronics would be released.”

r/gaming commenter · June 2026Source 26

This generation went the other way

I bought my PS5 in 2022 for $500. Four years later, the same console costs $650. I bought a Switch 2 at launch for $450. A year later, it’s $500.

The Xbox Series S matters most here. It was the budget console of this generation, the one a family like mine would have bought, and it now costs $200 more than it did at launch. Its 1TB version went from $449.99 to $599.99. “Paying $600 for a Series S just seems profoundly stupid,” one user wrote, in one of the thread’s most upvoted comments, with about 1,700 upvotes. Replying to someone who remembered $200 holiday sales, a parent wrote: “Ya I got 3 for my kids, 200 each.”26

The used market has turned upside down too. One user said someone tried to report them for listing their used PS5 at a “suspiciously low price,” even though “it was still like 100 USD more from the original purchase price.”26

What about inflation? Adjusted for inflation, the PS5’s $499.99 launch price comes to about $640 today14, so today’s $649.99 is roughly its launch price in real terms. Even so, the console never got cheaper. The PS2’s $299 launch price is about $570 in today’s money, and by 2006 you could buy one for $129, about $210 today. Six years into the PS5’s life, the cheap version never came. Even counting inflation, I’d pay about $85 more for my PS5 today than I did in 2022.

Why it’s happening: the AI boom

OpenAI released ChatGPT on November 30, 202215, and AI developers have been racing to buy computing power ever since. In September 2025, OpenAI and NVIDIA announced plans for at least 10 gigawatts of NVIDIA systems, which means millions of GPUs16. A month later, OpenAI signed a separate deal with AMD for 6 gigawatts of AMD GPUs17. Anthropic announced plans to use as many as one million of Google’s TPU chips18 and committed to buying $30 billion of computing capacity on Microsoft Azure19. These are announced plans and commitments. None of the companies has published how many chips have actually been delivered.

The money has followed. In fiscal 2022, NVIDIA earned more from gaming than from data centers. In fiscal 2026, its data-center revenue was $193.7 billion and its gaming revenue was $16 billion, about twelve to one20. NVIDIA still sells plenty to gamers, and its gaming revenue grew 41% last year, but its biggest customers are now AI companies.

The real squeeze is memory

AI isn’t buying up the chips inside your PS5. Consoles run on custom processors, not the AI chips NVIDIA sells to data centers. The shortage that matters is memory, the RAM and storage that every console, PC and data-center server needs.

AI chips use a special kind of memory called HBM, stacked right next to the processor. It’s made in the same factories as ordinary memory, but Micron says it takes about three times as much silicon to make the same amount28. Every wafer a memory maker moves to HBM yields about a third as much memory as it would have. Consoles use different memory from PCs (the PS5 and Xbox use GDDR6, for example), but it’s all made by the same few companies in the same factories. When those companies chase AI orders, everything else gets scarcer.

In December 2025, Micron announced it would shut down Crucial, its consumer brand, after nearly 30 years of selling RAM and SSDs to PC builders. The company said the move would “improve supply and support for our larger, strategic customers in faster-growing segments”21, meaning AI data centers. One 32GB DDR5 memory kit on Amazon went from $73.50 in April 2025 to $277.98 on the day of the announcement22. Tom’s Hardware now reports that the cheapest 32GB DDR5 kits cost $375 or more24. For anyone building a gaming PC, memory went from an afterthought to one of the most expensive parts of the build. On September 30, 2026, the research firm TrendForce forecast another 10 to 15% rise in DRAM contract prices in the fourth quarter, with suppliers giving priority to server products23.

The hardware makers feel it too. In June 2026, Microsoft said console memory and storage costs had risen more than 2.5 times and that it expected them to double again by fall 202712. Nintendo’s president, Shuntaro Furukawa, named memory costs as the main reason for the Switch 2 increase and said even the higher price won’t cover all of Nintendo’s added costs25. Valve gave the same reason when it raised Steam Deck prices in May27.

AI isn’t the whole story, though. Console prices had stopped falling years before the memory shortage, because shrinking the chip has mostly stopped saving money29. When Sony released a slimmer PS5 in 2023, the disc version stayed at $499.99 and the digital version went up $5030. The first U.S. price increases on existing consoles came in 2025, as new tariffs took effect. Sony cited a “challenging economic environment” when it raised prices that August9, and “continued pressures in the global economic landscape” in 202610. It has never blamed memory directly. Tariffs, a weaker yen, inflation and bigger, costlier games all add to the bill.

Not every gamer buys the cost explanation. “It’s not just AI, it’s corporate greed,” one user wrote. “They can very easily capitalize on the hysteria and increase prices just because.” Others pushed back. One argued that “the hardware costs are so high it was unsustainable to keep selling them at a loss.”26 Both can be true at once. Memory costs are real, and a shortage also makes it easier to raise prices without much backlash.

Is gaming becoming a luxury again?

You can still play cheaply. Mobile games, indie titles, free-to-play games and older hardware are all still there. Cloud services can even stream new games without a console, though they run in the same kind of data centers AI companies are competing for. But buying current-generation hardware costs more every year, and the budget console now costs what a premium one used to.

For many lower- and middle-income households, the choice was never between a $500 console and an $800 one. It’s between a console and rent or groceries. Hardware is only part of the cost, too. Mario Kart World launched at $79.9931, and Xbox Game Pass Ultimate went from $19.99 to $29.99 a month in October 202532. Some players are already giving up. “Honestly at this point I’ll probably just play with what I got and when the time comes I’ll just find another hobby lmao,” one user wrote26.

The sales numbers suggest they’re not alone. In August 2026, Americans bought 559,000 video game consoles, 15% fewer than a year earlier and the fewest for any August since 2013, according to the research firm Circana. The average price paid hit an August record of $54133. If prices keep rising, gaming risks splitting into people who can afford current hardware and people who can’t.

There’s no sign of relief yet. Microsoft expects memory costs to keep climbing through 2027, and Nintendo has said its latest increase still doesn’t cover what it’s paying. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron are all building new memory factories, but the first ones aren’t due to start production until 2027, and most of the new capacity arrives in 2028 or later34.

What could help

  • Governments

    Governments can fund new chip and memory factories and support competition among chip suppliers. Extra capacity helps every buyer, including the AI companies themselves.

  • Regulators

    Regulators can check whether exclusive supply deals or market concentration are unfairly locking up critical parts. Buying chips in bulk isn’t illegal, so the focus should be proven market abuse, not stopping AI companies from growing.

  • Console makers

    Console makers can support older hardware for longer, keep backward compatibility, sell affordable refurbished units and keep selling physical games.

  • Gamers

    Gamers have options too. Refurbished consoles, last generation’s hardware and secondhand physical games are still the cheapest way to play. A PS4 or an original Switch still runs thousands of great games.

Who gets to play

  • Nintendo 64 Launch price $199 The way I got in 1999, cut to $99

By most measures the industry is fine. Revenue keeps rising and there are more players than ever. The part that’s in trouble is the cheap way in.

That older, cheaper Nintendo 64 was how I got in. Gaming is something I truly value. It’s how I relieve stress and immerse myself in a whole new world. I’m fortunate enough to be able to afford these consoles now, but I think about the kids who are like I was, growing up in lower- and middle-income families. How are they supposed to afford to game? Will this continue until only the rich can afford to play? Memory costs are real, but companies still choose how much of them to pass on to players.

Somewhere right now, a kid is playing the cheap console their friends traded up from. I want there to still be a console like that in ten years.
